The Dust Bowl

Environment, Policy and Migration on the Southern Plains (1931-1939)
Prompt: Evaluate the extent to which the Dust Bowl was a natural disaster, a man-made agricultural catastrophe, or a federal-policy failure. Use at least six of the nine documents and the quantitative data to support your argument.

Historical Context

Between 1931 and 1939, the southern Great Plains - the Oklahoma and Texas panhandles, southeastern Colorado, southwestern Kansas, and northeastern New Mexico - became the most ecologically devastated region in modern American history. A drought that began in 1930 met soils that had been stripped of native sod by twenty years of mechanized wheat farming. The result was airborne topsoil, "black blizzards" that buried farms, and the largest internal migration in American history to that point.

The disaster was not strictly natural. Between 1914 and 1929, wheat prices and government incentives during World War I drove the "great plow-up" of the southern plains. Tractors and one-way disc plows broke open millions of acres of marginal grassland that ecologists later argued should never have been cultivated. When the rains failed in 1931, there was nothing left to hold the soil down.

The federal response was unprecedented in scope. Hugh Hammond Bennett, often called the father of soil conservation, testified before the Senate on April 2, 1935 - timed so that a real Washington dust storm rolled in during his testimony, transporting Plains topsoil to the windows of the Capitol. Congress passed the Soil Conservation Act eighteen days later. The Soil Conservation Service (SCS), the Agricultural Adjustment Administration (AAA), the Civilian Conservation Corps (CCC), and the Prairie States Forestry Project (the Shelterbelt) reshaped the landscape and the legal relationship between farmers and their land. About 3.5 million people left the plains; most stayed.

Key Vocabulary

Great Plow-Up: The 1914-1929 mechanized cultivation of millions of acres of southern-plains grassland for wheat, encouraged by wartime grain prices.
Black Blizzard: A dust storm so dense it blacked out daylight. Black Sunday (April 14, 1935) is the most famous.
Dust Pneumonia: Lung damage from inhaled fine dust; killed children and elderly across the plains in 1935-1936.
Soil Conservation Service (SCS): Federal agency created April 1935 under Hugh Bennett; promoted contour plowing, terracing, and the Shelterbelt.
AAA: Agricultural Adjustment Administration. Paid farmers to reduce production; in practice often paid landowners and displaced tenants.
Shelterbelt: 1934-1942 federal project that planted ~220 million trees in a 100-mile-wide belt from the Dakotas to Texas to break wind.
Okie: Pejorative term for Dust Bowl migrants to California, regardless of state of origin. About 3.5 million migrated; most stayed.

Annotated Source Analysis (the "what good looks like")

Doc A (Fleeing a Dust Storm): Strong students notice the staging - Rothstein later admitted he asked the younger son to lean further into the wind. Compare to Doc C (companion frame) where the figures stand more upright. The iconic frame became iconic because of editorial selection. Sourcing matters: the FSA's brief was advocacy for New Deal programs, so the photograph is documentary AND propagandistic. Both are true.

Model SOAPSTone for Doc A (Rothstein):

  • Speaker: Arthur Rothstein, FSA staff photographer; the captioned voice is the agency, not the family. Arthur Coble and his sons appear in the frame but do not narrate.
  • Occasion: April 1936, Cimarron County, Oklahoma, peak Dust Bowl year; FSA-Stryker assignment to document plains conditions for Resettlement Administration use.
  • Audience: Roy Stryker's FSA editorial unit first; secondarily Congress, the press, and the urban public whose support New Deal agricultural programs needed.
  • Purpose: To produce a single legible image of Dust Bowl human cost that would justify continued federal intervention. The staging (asking the youngest son to lean further into the wind) reveals the advocacy purpose under the documentary form.
  • Subject: A father and two sons walking against blowing topsoil toward a half-buried shed; literally, the family; analytically, the human cost of the great plow-up.
  • Tone: Stark, near-cinematic; sympathetic but unsentimental. The composition forces the viewer to read the figures as victims of a force, not agents of it.

Doc D (Tractored Out): The single most analytically rich image in the set. Furrows run directly up to the abandoned tenant house. Lange's caption explicitly named the AAA paradox: federal payments to landowners financed the mechanization that displaced tenants. Strong students will use Doc D as evidence for both Bucket 1 (mechanized land misuse) AND Bucket 2 (federal-policy failure) and treat that double-fitting as the argument. (See full Doc D model SOAPSTone in the print answer key, Tab 7.)

Doc F (Pie Town): The counter-evidence document. Russell Lee's Pie Town series shows homesteaders practicing the kind of small-scale, conservation-aware farming the SCS promoted. Critical for any thesis arguing federal response had successes as well as failures. Without F, the argument over-determines failure.

Tab 3 Wheat Acreage Chart: The decisive quantitative evidence for the man-made thesis. Acreage roughly tripled between 1914 and 1929. The drought of 1931 met soil that had no native sod left to hold it. Strong students cite the chart explicitly.

Sensitive Topic Guidance

The FSA photographs were commissioned propaganda. This is not a criticism - it is a historical fact that strengthens analysis. The Resettlement Administration (later FSA) hired Roy Stryker to manage a documentary photography unit whose explicit purpose was to build public support for New Deal agricultural programs. The photographs are real, the suffering they document is real, and the editorial selection is also real. Teach all three.

The Dust Bowl is frequently misremembered. Of the roughly 2.5 million people who left the Great Plains states during the 1930s, only about 200,000 ended up in California. Most who left went to nearby cities; most southern-plains residents stayed. The "Okie" experience in California was real but is often universalized. Steinbeck's Grapes of Wrath (1939) and Lange's photographs together created a public memory more uniform than the historical reality.

The Dust Bowl was concurrent with - but distinct from - Black sharecropper displacement in the Cotton Belt. Both involved AAA-payment-driven evictions of tenant farmers in the 1930s, but the Cotton Belt mechanism was racialized credit and labor relations in the Deep South, while the southern plains mechanism was wheat mechanization in a majority-white tenant population. These two histories are frequently conflated in classroom narratives. Doc D is southern-plains, not Cotton Belt.

Recommended scholarship for teacher background:

  • Donald Worster, Dust Bowl: The Southern Plains in the 1930s (1979). The canonical environmental-history account; aligns with the man-made-catastrophe thesis.
  • Timothy Egan, The Worst Hard Time (2006). National Book Award-winning narrative history of the people who stayed; corrective to the migration-centered popular memory.
  • James N. Gregory, American Exodus: The Dust Bowl Migration and Okie Culture in California (1989). The migration scholarship.
Further Reading

Five canonical histories that shape professional debate over Dust Bowl causation. Worster and Bonnifield (both 1979) frame the central historiographic argument: was it primarily ecological misuse or primarily an extension of the Great Depression?

  • Donald Worster, Dust Bowl: The Southern Plains in the 1930s (1979, rev. 2004). Bancroft Prize. Argues capitalism's "ethos of unrestrained growth" applied to a fragile grassland produced the disaster. Foundational for the man-made-catastrophe thesis.
  • Paul Bonnifield, The Dust Bowl: Men, Dirt, and Depression (1979). The counter to Worster. Argues plains farmers were resilient and capable, that the disaster was primarily climatic and economic, and that federal narratives of farmer incompetence were unfair. Required reading for any student arguing the natural-disaster thesis.
  • R. Douglas Hurt, The Dust Bowl: An Agricultural and Social History (1981). The middle path. Detailed agricultural history that takes both Worster and Bonnifield seriously and grounds the argument in documented farming practice and SCS records.
  • Timothy Egan, The Worst Hard Time (2006). National Book Award. Narrative history of the families who stayed; corrective to Steinbeck-shaped public memory. Highly accessible for students.
  • James N. Gregory, American Exodus: The Dust Bowl Migration and Okie Culture in California (1989). The migration scholarship: who actually left, where they went, what happened to them after California.

Discussion Questions
  1. The Dust Bowl is often called a "natural disaster." Based on the wheat-acreage chart and Doc D, is that label accurate?
  2. Rothstein admitted asking the boy in Doc A to lean further into the wind. Does that staging undermine the photograph as evidence, or is it itself evidence about the FSA project?
  3. About 3.5 million people left the Plains during the 1930s, but most southern-plains residents stayed. Why do migration narratives (Doc I, Steinbeck) dominate public memory of the era?
  4. Hugh Bennett's 1935 Senate testimony was perfectly timed with a real dust storm hitting Washington. Does that timing strengthen or weaken his argument for the Soil Conservation Act?
  5. The Ogallala Aquifer that today irrigates much of the southern plains is being depleted faster than it recharges. Did the federal response of 1935-1942 actually solve the underlying problem, or just postpone it?

Teacher Answer Key

Model SOAPSTone (Doc D - Tractored Out)

  • Speaker: Dorothea Lange, FSA photographer (the captioned voice). The displaced tenant family is absent from the frame - that absence is the subject.
  • Occasion: June 1938, Childress County, Texas; mid-Dust-Bowl AAA implementation period.
  • Audience: FSA / Resettlement Administration; eventually Congress, the press, and the American public.
  • Purpose: To make the AAA paradox visible - federal payments financing the displacement of the tenants the program was supposed to help.
  • Subject: Tractor furrows running directly up to and around an empty tenant house; mechanized power agriculture replacing tenant labor.
  • Tone: Quietly indictment-laced; documentary in form, advocacy in selection.

Model Thesis

The Dust Bowl was fundamentally a man-made agricultural catastrophe. Drought triggered it (Doc B, Tab 3 storm chart), but the airborne topsoil was the direct consequence of the 1914-1929 great plow-up (Tab 3 wheat-acreage chart). Federal policy deepened the crisis through the AAA's perverse incentives (Docs D, E) even as the Soil Conservation Service began rehabilitation (Doc F).

Common Misconceptions to Address

  • "The Dust Bowl was a natural disaster." It wasn't, primarily. Drought was the trigger; the great plow-up was the cause of the airborne soil.
  • "Most Plains residents migrated to California." About 3.5 million left the Plains across the 1930s; only roughly 200,000 reached California. Most stayed in their region.
  • "The FSA photographs are neutral documentation." They were commissioned by the Resettlement Administration to build public support for New Deal programs. They are documentary AND advocacy. Both at once.
  • "The federal response solved the problem." The 1939-1941 rains and WWII grain demand bailed out the Plains. The Ogallala Aquifer depletion in our own time suggests the underlying problem of unsuited cultivation may not have been solved, only postponed.
