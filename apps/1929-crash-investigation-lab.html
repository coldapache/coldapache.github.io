How to Teach This

6 Teaching Strategies

  1. Start with the chart. Display the Dow Jones chart and ask: "What do you see? What might explain these changes?" Let students hypothesize before investigating.
  2. Jigsaw the stations. Assign each group 1-2 stations. Groups become experts on their topic and present findings to the class before the final assessment.
  3. Connect to today. Before Station 5, have students brainstorm: "Could a crash like this happen today?" Then let the station provide the answer.
  4. Use the margin buying math. Walk through the math of margin buying on the board: $1,000 invested, $9,000 borrowed, stock drops 10%. Students calculate the loss. This makes the mechanics concrete.
  5. Primary source close reading. Use the Hoover quotes and newspaper headlines for a mini-lesson on sourcing and perspective. Why did leaders say what they said?
  6. Financial literacy bridge. End with a class discussion: "What lessons from 1929 apply to your personal finances today?" Great transition to a personal finance unit.

Economics Primer for Non-Economics Teachers

Key Concepts Students Need

Margin Buying: Purchasing stocks with borrowed money. In the 1920s, investors could buy stocks with just 10% down and borrow the rest from brokers. If the stock price rose, great. If it fell, the broker demanded immediate repayment (a "margin call"), forcing the investor to sell at a loss.

Speculation: Buying assets not for their underlying value but because you expect the price to keep rising. By 1929, millions of Americans were buying stocks they knew nothing about, assuming prices would go up forever.

Bubble Dynamics: A bubble forms when asset prices far exceed their actual value, driven by optimism and herd behavior. Everyone buys because everyone else is buying. The longer it inflates, the worse the pop.

Liquidity: How easily assets can be converted to cash. During a crash, everyone tries to sell at once, but there are no buyers. Prices collapse because liquidity evaporates.

Federal Reserve Policy: The Fed controls interest rates and money supply. In 1928-29, the Fed raised rates to cool speculation, but this also squeezed businesses. After the crash, the Fed failed to expand the money supply, worsening the Depression.

Bank Runs: When depositors lose confidence in a bank and rush to withdraw their money. Since banks loan out most deposits, they cannot pay everyone at once, leading to bank failure. This was pre-FDIC, so there was no deposit insurance.

Historical Context: The Roaring Twenties

The 1920s saw genuine economic growth. Automobiles, radio, electricity, and consumer goods transformed American life. Industrial production doubled. The middle class expanded. But beneath the prosperity lay deep structural problems: farmers were in depression throughout the decade, wealth inequality reached extreme levels, consumer debt soared, and the stock market became a casino where shoeshine boys and taxi drivers traded tips on stocks they could not afford.

It is important that students understand: the 1920s were not fake prosperity. Real innovation occurred. But the financial system built on top of that real growth was fragile, speculative, and unregulated. The crash did not cause the Depression by itself. The Depression resulted from the interaction between the crash and pre-existing weaknesses in banking, agriculture, trade policy, and income distribution.

Connection to 2008 Financial Crisis

Students will naturally ask "Could this happen again?" The answer is: it did, in 2008. The parallels are striking:

  • 1929: Stocks bought on margin. 2008: Houses bought with subprime mortgages.
  • 1929: Everyone believed stocks only go up. 2008: Everyone believed housing prices only go up.
  • 1929: Regulators looked the other way. 2008: Regulators looked the other way.
  • 1929: Complex financial instruments (investment trusts). 2008: Complex financial instruments (mortgage-backed securities, CDOs).
  • Key difference: In 2008, the Fed and government intervened aggressively (bailouts, stimulus). In 1929-32, they did not. This is why 2008 produced a recession, not a depression.

Primary Sources

1. New York Times, October 25, 1929

"WORST STOCK CRASH STEMMED BY BANKS; 12,894,650-Share Day Swamps Market. LEADERS CONFER, FIND CONDITIONS SOUND."

Context: This was the day after Black Thursday. Note the headline's emphasis on bankers' confidence. The "leaders" were five major bankers who pooled money to buy stocks and halt the panic. It worked for exactly two days.

2. Jesse Livermore, Wall Street Trader

"There is nothing new in Wall Street. There can't be because speculation is as old as the hills. Whatever happens in the stock market today has happened before and will happen again."

Context: Livermore was known as the "Great Bear of Wall Street." He made $100 million (over $1.5 billion today) by short-selling during the 1929 crash. His quote underscores the cyclical nature of financial panics.

3. Herbert Hoover, March 1930

"The fundamental business of the country, that is, production and distribution of commodities, is on a sound and prosperous basis... We have now passed the worst."

Context: Hoover said this four months after the crash. Unemployment was already rising rapidly. The worst was far from over. This quote illustrates how leaders underestimated the crisis and prioritized confidence over accuracy.

4. John Kenneth Galbraith, "The Great Crash 1929" (1955)

"The sense of responsibility in the financial community for the community as a whole is not small. It is nearly nil. Perhaps this is inherent. In a community where the weights are given to what people own rather than what people are, it is inevitable."

Context: Galbraith's book remains the definitive account of the crash. Written 26 years later with full historical perspective, it examines how greed, groupthink, and lack of accountability enabled the disaster.

Discussion Questions with Sample Answers

1. Why did so many ordinary Americans invest in stocks during the 1920s, even people who had never invested before?
Several factors: margin buying made stocks accessible to people without much capital. Success stories in newspapers created FOMO. The widespread availability of "investment trusts" (early mutual funds) made the market seem safe. And genuine economic growth made optimism feel rational, not reckless.
2. Was the Federal Reserve more responsible for causing the crash or for making the Depression worse?
Arguably more responsible for the Depression. The Fed raised rates in 1928-29 to cool speculation, which contributed to the crash but was defensible policy. However, its failure to expand the money supply after the crash (letting it contract by 1/3) and failure to rescue failing banks turned a stock market correction into a systemic collapse. Milton Friedman and Ben Bernanke both argued the Fed's inaction was the single biggest cause of the Depression's severity.
3. How does the 1929 crash compare to the 2008 financial crisis? What was the most important difference in outcome?
Both involved speculative bubbles built on borrowed money (stocks/margin in 1929, housing/mortgages in 2008), both featured inadequate regulation, and both spread globally. The key difference was the government response: in 2008, the Fed and Treasury intervened with massive bailouts and stimulus. In 1929-32, Hoover's government did comparatively little. This difference largely explains why 2008 produced a deep recession but not a depression.
4. Why did the Smoot-Hawley Tariff make the Depression worse rather than better?
Smoot-Hawley raised tariffs on over 20,000 imported goods, aiming to protect American industries. But other countries retaliated with their own tariffs, collapsing international trade by 66% between 1929 and 1934. American exporters (especially farmers) lost their overseas markets. The tariff turned a domestic crisis into a global one.
5. What is the strongest argument that the crash was inevitable? What is the strongest argument that it could have been prevented?
Inevitable: speculative bubbles have occurred throughout history and always burst. When stock prices are 10x higher than justified by actual company earnings, a correction is mathematically certain. Preventable: the Fed could have raised margin requirements gradually starting in 1927, forcing investors to put up more cash. This would have cooled speculation without crashing the market. The bubble could have deflated rather than popped.
6. What lesson from 1929 do you think is most relevant to your generation's financial decisions?
Open-ended, but strong answers might reference: the dangers of investing borrowed money, the illusion that prices always go up, herd mentality in investment (today: crypto, meme stocks), the importance of diversification, or the role of regulation in protecting ordinary people from financial system failures.

Pacing Guide

3-Day Plan

Day 1 (50 min): Introduction with Dow Jones chart discussion (10 min). Students work Stations 1-2 in pairs (30 min). Share findings and margin buying math exercise (10 min).

Day 2 (50 min): Quick review of Day 1 findings (5 min). Students work Stations 3-4 (30 min). Class discussion on crash timeline and human impact (15 min).

Day 3 (50 min): Station 5 on reforms (15 min). 2008 comparison mini-lesson (10 min). Final argument builder (15 min). Share and discuss theses (10 min).

1-Day Condensed (90 min block)

Chart intro (10 min). Jigsaw all 5 stations (30 min). Expert group presentations (20 min). 2008 connection discussion (10 min). Argument builder (15 min). Share out (5 min).

Differentiation

Struggling learners: Pre-teach margin buying with a concrete example (buying a $100 item with $10 down). Focus on Stations 1 and 3 (most narrative, least abstract). Provide sentence starters for the argument builder.

Advanced learners: Have them research the Pecora Commission hearings of 1933 and write a brief on what investigators found. Challenge them to evaluate whether Glass-Steagall's 1999 repeal contributed to the 2008 crisis.

ELL students: Pre-teach vocabulary with visuals. The Dow Jones chart and station artifacts provide visual scaffolding. Pair with a partner for the argument builder.
