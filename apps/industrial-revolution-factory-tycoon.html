Northbridge Cotton Mill, Lancashire

You are the new owner of a small textile mill in 1820. Steam power is replacing handlooms. Cotton from American plantations and Indian looms feeds an exploding global market. You inherited the mill and £5,000 in working capital. Your goal: survive thirty years of boom, depression, riots, and reform, and judge how you wish to be remembered.

How to play. Each turn is one year. Use the Workforce, Capital, and Laws tabs to make decisions. When you are ready, press End the Year. The mill will produce, sell, and react. New laws will arrive on the calendar and bind you whether you like them or not.

Period money is in pounds (£). 1 pound is roughly two weeks of skilled adult wages in 1820.

What Reform Looks Like

Most reform laws begin with a Royal Commission interviewing workers. Their testimony shocks Parliament. A bill passes. An inspector visits.

Owners who plan ahead beat the law. Owners who break it draw fines, convictions, and ruined reputations.

Teacher's Guide

Activity: Industrial Revolution Factory Tycoon (1820 - 1850)
Grades: 7 - 12   |   Time: 45 - 70 minutes

Learning Targets

  • Trace how British factory legislation evolved from the 1819 Cotton Mills Act through the 1847 Ten Hours Act.
  • Weigh the trade-offs faced by mill owners between profit, reputation, and worker welfare.
  • Explain why labor reform happened in waves driven by Royal Commissions, journalism, and strikes.
  • Describe the role of child labor in early industry and the social arguments that ended it.

How the Simulation Works

Students inherit a Lancashire cotton mill in 1820 with £5,000 and 20 adult workers. Each turn is one year (30 turns). Students set wages and hours, hire or fire workers, invest in machinery, ventilation, safety, schooling, and housing, and respond to events. Real laws arrive on real dates and bind the player.

Suggested Pacing

  1. Hook (5 min): Show a primary source quote from the 1832 Sadler Committee. Ask: would you have hired children?
  2. Decade 1: 1820 - 1829 (10 min): Students set up. Watch wages, hours, child labor.
  3. Decade 2: 1830 - 1839 (10 min): 1833 Factory Act lands. Discuss the cost of compliance versus the cost of fines.
  4. Decade 3: 1840 - 1850 (10 min): Ten Hours Movement, 1847 Act, 1848 Public Health Act. Strikes peak.
  5. Debrief (15 min): Reveal endings. Compare student verdicts. Ask: who profited from reform?

Discussion Questions

  • Did you keep child workers after 1833? Why? What was the cost-benefit?
  • How did your morale and reputation change when you raised wages? Was it worth it?
  • Whose voices are missing from this simulation? (Workers, women, abolitionists, mill children themselves.)
  • Compare your mill to Robert Owen's New Lanark. What did Owen do that you did or did not do?

Five Key Acts (Reference)

  • 1819 Cotton Mills Act - no children under 9 in cotton mills, 12-hour limit for under-16. Weakly enforced.
  • 1825 Combination Act - workers may form unions for wage bargaining (limited).
  • 1833 Factory Act - first paid factory inspectors, ages 9-13 limited to 9 hours, 14-18 to 12 hours.
  • 1844 Factory Act - women included in 12-hour limit, machine guards required.
  • 1847 Ten Hours Act - 10-hour day for women and 13-18 year olds.

Differentiation

Support: Pair students. Provide a simplified ledger. Ask only for one decade.
Extension: Have students replay aiming for the Reformer ending. Then write a letter from a worker's perspective.
Assessment: Final ending screen + 1-page reflection citing at least two laws.

Endings

  • Robber Baron: high cash, low reputation, ignored reform.
  • Industrial Pioneer: balanced cash and reputation, paid the cost of compliance.
  • Mill Reformer: low cash, high reputation, modeled on Robert Owen.
  • Bankrupt: ran out of cash before 1850.
  • Convicted Owner: repeat law-breaker, fined and imprisoned.
